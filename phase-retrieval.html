Part 1 · Simulator

The scan geometry

The SkyScan 1272 has a small X-ray spot and no gratings. X-rays bend slightly at boundaries inside the sample, and over the gap between the sample and the camera that bending turns into bright and dark lines along every edge. That gap is what the phase effect depends on. The sliders on the left take their values from a scan's .log file; the sample in this part is simulated soft tissue.

Why one number fixes it

Phase effects boost fine detail: the smaller the feature, the stronger the boost (amber). The Paganin filter reduces fine detail by the opposite amount (teal). When δ/β matches the material, the two cancel and the result is flat (black). Set it too low and halos survive; set it too high and real detail is smoothed away.

In the code: flyFilter(), using fft2() on a 512 × 512 mirrored copy

Same filter, in two directions. On a real projection, fringes run along edges in every direction, so the filter works on rows and columns together. The image is first mirrored at its borders so the filter doesn't mix the left edge with the right edge. Then each pattern is divided by the same factor as in the simulator, using both its horizontal and vertical size.

Reading these results
  • The phase fringe in this scan is weak: about 3% against 1% noise at the sharpest edge of the head, and only 0.21% averaged over all clean edges (Part 3, Test 5). Inference That is expected for 8.15 µm pixels at 100 kV: a Fresnel number near 37 means fringes are thin compared with a pixel.
  • On this projection the rim at the head edge disappears only at δ/β ≈ 2000. Averaged over clean edges in 180 projections, the fringe disappears at δ/β ≈ 580. A single sharp edge overstates what the whole sample needs, by about 3× here.
  • Unverified The 30 keV effective energy is an estimate for 100 kV with no filter. A different assumed energy rescales the δ/β number for the same filter: a program assuming energy E needs δ/β × E / 30 keV. Part 5 explains how to carry the value over to NRecon.

  • This is one projection. In a reconstruction the same filter is applied to all 1800 projections first. Part 3 measures what that does to the slices.
Part 3 · Case study

Does phase retrieval help a real scan?

Parts 1 and 2 show what the filter does. Here we measure whether it improves the reconstruction of the same fly scan, or only smooths it. We reconstructed the scan more than a hundred times on a GPU with different settings and measured every result without needing a true image.

The short answer

  1. The phase effect in this scan is real but small. Just outside the fly's edges, the projections are on average only 0.21% brighter than the open beam (about 3% at the sharpest edge of the head, as in Part 2).
  2. The δ/β that cancels the measured fringe is about 580, assuming a 30 keV effective energy. Higher values do not correct anything further; they only smooth.
  3. On this scan, Paganin filtering behaves almost exactly like smoothing the projections. At equal noise it keeps about as much fine detail as Gaussian smoothing. Its one measurable advantage is a smaller dark halo at edges (3.6% against about 6.5% at the same noise).
  4. The noise reduction is real and useful. δ/β = 600 lowers noise 2.4× (contrast-to-noise 68 instead of 30); δ/β = 1600 lowers it 4× (CNR 109). The cost is fine detail: structures with a 40 µm period keep 29% or 14% of their strength.
  5. No combination escapes the trade-off. All 60 combinations of phase filter, reconstruction filter, Paganin variant and number of projections fall on one "less noise for less detail" curve.

Why the effect is weak here

For X-rays, a material's refractive index is n = 1 − δ + iβ. β describes absorption, which conventional CT measures; δ describes the phase shift. For soft tissue at tens of keV, δ is roughly a thousand times larger than β. A detector records only intensity, so the phase shows up only after the beam has travelled some distance past the sample, as the bright and dark fringe lines simulated in Part 1.

The Fresnel number compares the pixel size with the width of that fringe, which is about √(λ·zeff):

λ(30 keV) = 0.0413 nm · √(λ·z_eff) = √(0.0413 nm × 43.0 mm) ≈ 1.3 µm · N_F = p² / (λ·z_eff) = 8.15² / 1.78 ≈ 37

The fringe is about six times narrower than a pixel, so each pixel averages most of it away. Phase contrast becomes strong when NF approaches 1: smaller pixels, longer propagation distances or lower energies. Synchrotron imaging and high-resolution lab systems work in that regime; a binned 8 µm scan at 100 kV does not. The finite focal spot blurs the fringe further.

Lesson · only δ/β × λ matters

The Paganin filter depends on δ/β and the wavelength only through their product. δ/β = 1067 at 20 keV, 1600 at 30 keV and 2133 at 40 keV give the identical filter; we checked this numerically. A lab tube emits a broad spectrum, so the energy a program assumes is a convention, and the δ/β number it shows changes with it. δ/β values cannot be compared between programs unless the assumed energy is known.

Phase retrieval, or just smoothing?

The Paganin filter is a low-pass filter, and any low-pass filter lowers noise and blurs. A claim that phase retrieval improves an image therefore needs a control: the same projections smoothed with an ordinary filter, compared at equal noise.

Where phase contrast is strong, the two give very different images. Propagation has turned fine structure into large fringes, well above the noise. Smoothing leaves blurred fringes, which are wrong grey values at every boundary. Paganin's filter is built as the inverse of that propagation: it folds the fringe signal back into place, so the result is quantitatively correct and far less noisy than an absorption-only image at the same dose and resolution. Kitchen et al. (2017) report CT dose reductions of several orders of magnitude on this basis.

On this scan the fringe is only 0.21% of the beam. There is almost nothing to fold back, so the filter has little to do except smooth. The scan sits in the weak-phase regime, and our measurements show that it does not get the benefit seen in the strong-phase regime.

Lesson · choose a control that can tell the methods apart

Paganin and Gaussian filters are both linear and act on the same data. At equal noise they must keep similar amounts of detail, whatever the physics; only the filter shapes differ. The measurement that separates them is how well each removes the fringe and its halo, because only Paganin is designed to do that. Here that difference is real but small.

How we measured quality without a true image

We wrote our own GPU reconstruction in Python with PyTorch, rather than using NRecon, so that we could try many settings and measure each the same way. It is a cone-beam FDK reconstruction (Feldkamp, Davis and Kress, 1984), the family of method NRecon uses, built from the raw projections and the scan's .log file. Each setting took about 20 seconds for four test slices: 498 (abdomen and wing), 594 (abdomen and thorax), 690 (thorax with flight muscle) and 786 (head).

Every setting was reconstructed twice: once from the even-numbered projections and once from the odd-numbered ones. Both halves show the same fly with independent noise. Their difference contains only noise; their agreement measures signal.

Lesson · not every standard metric fits the question

Fourier ring correlation (FRC), the usual resolution measure for split-data reconstructions, cannot see blur from a linear filter. It normalises each frequency ring, and a filter multiplies signal and noise in a ring by the same factor, so the ratio does not change. We also dropped edge width and overshoot, because insect tissue has no flat interior to measure them against.

Tests 1 and 2 · groundwork

Matching the vendor's geometry, and removing rings

Why
Every later number depends on correct geometry, and ring artefacts would be counted as noise.
What we did
We reconstructed slice 690 with many centre-of-rotation shifts, both rotation directions, three detector-tilt values and neighbouring projection rows, and compared each with NRecon's slice. A conservation test checked the units: the total attenuation in a slice must equal the total in the matching projection row. For rings we used a correction modelled on NRecon's, one map per block of 258 projections.
What we found
  • The best match is at a centre-of-rotation shift of 29.5 px, exactly NRecon's recorded post-alignment, with counter-clockwise rotation. Our first attempt used the wrong direction; doubled edges revealed it.
  • NRecon numbers sections from the bottom of the projection (row = 1343 − section). Detector tilt made no measurable difference.
  • Our values are in mm⁻¹ and pass the conservation test. NRecon's exported BMP grey scale is about 8× smaller, so absolute values here should not be compared with NRecon's files.
  • Away from the steel pin, our unfiltered reconstruction has the same noise relative to contrast as NRecon's. Near the pin NRecon's is lower, thanks to its beam-hardening correction, which we did not implement.
  • NRecon's ring setting (radius 9) reduces rings 5×, leaving them 16× weaker than random noise. We used it everywhere.
Test 3

A δ/β sweep: what each strength does

We reconstructed the four slices with δ/β from 50 to 25 600, doubling at each step. Each doubling cuts noise by about a third, and the smallest surviving detail grows. The dark halo reaches zero near δ/β = 3200, more than five times the fringe-matched value from Test 5. At that point the halo is being blurred away, not corrected, which suggests that most of the halo in the slices has causes other than phase.

Test 4 · the control

Is Paganin better than plain smoothing?

Why
If simple smoothing does the same job, phase retrieval adds nothing for this scan.
What we did
We repeated the sweep with Gaussian smoothing of the projections (σ = 0.5 to 6 px) in place of the Paganin filter, similar to NRecon's Smoothing option, and compared the two families at equal noise.
What we found
  • The curves of detail kept and of thin-wall contrast lie almost on top of each other (left and right panels below).
  • The shapes differ slightly: Paganin keeps a little more of the finest detail and removes a little more at 100–300 µm.
  • Paganin removes the dark halo more completely at the same noise (middle panel): 3.6% at δ/β = 1600 against about 6.5% for Gaussian smoothing.
Test 5

Measuring the fringe directly

The idea
Absorption can never make a pixel brighter than the open beam beside the sample. A bright rim just outside the fly's outline in a projection is therefore phase contrast by definition. Correct phase retrieval flattens that rim to the air level and goes no further.
What we did
In 180 projections (every 10th) we found the fly's outline, kept only edges facing clean air, and averaged the transmission at each distance from the edge, before and after each filter setting. Our first attempt used the whole outline and failed: wings and hairs lie just outside most of it.
What we found
The raw projections show a rim of +0.21%. Paganin filtering cancels it at δ/β ≈ 580 (30 keV), a smoothing length of 9 µm, about one pixel. At 1600 the edge is already pulled into the air (−0.5%); at 6400, to −2.1%. The single head edge in Part 2 needed about 2000: one sharp edge overstates what the sample as a whole needs.
Test 6

A multivariate grid

Why
To see whether any combination of settings beats the single-parameter trade-off.
What we did
60 reconstructions in all. The main grid of 48 crossed the phase filter (none, Paganin 600, 1600, 6400) with the reconstruction window (Ram-Lak, the sharpest; Shepp-Logan; Hamming, NRecon's default; Hann, the smoothest) and the number of projections (1800 as scanned; 900 and 450, which a 2× or 4× faster scan would give). Another 12 tested two Paganin variants: the generalised formula, and filtering after taking the logarithm.
What we found
  • One curve. All combinations fall on the same noise-versus-detail curve. Choosing settings means choosing a point on it.
  • With phase retrieval on, a sharper window pays off. Without Paganin, Ram-Lak costs 58% more noise than Hamming; with Paganin 600 it costs only 25% more and keeps 42% of 40 µm detail instead of 29%.
  • The Paganin variants differ from the standard method by only a few percent.
  • Fewer projections. Halving them raised noise 2.4×, not the 1.4× expected from photon statistics, because too few angles add streaks. Phase retrieval can bring a half-time scan back below today's noise (Paganin 1600 with 900 projections: 6.8 against 9.8), but only by giving up most detail below about 100 µm. With fewer projections the even/odd split overstates streaks, so this part is qualitative.
  • The halo depends only on the phase filter, not on the window or the number of projections, as expected for a physical effect.

Part 5 · Practice

Using phase retrieval in NRecon

  1. Check that the scan folder still has the raw projection images and the matching .log. Phase retrieval works on projections, so reconstructed slices alone are not enough.
  2. Open the dataset in NRecon 2.0 or newer. Bruker's distributor describes phase retrieval as new in 2.0. The fly scan was reconstructed with NRecon 2.1.0.2 on the facility PC, so the option should be there.
  3. Turn on phase retrieval and enter δ/β. The distances and pixel size come from the .log automatically. Unverified We could not confirm the menu name; Bruker method note MN133 has the walk-through.
  4. Choose δ/β from the fringe, not from the halo in the slices. The dark band along edges in reconstructed slices is mostly not phase contrast: it disappears only near δ/β 3200 (Test 3), about 5× past the fringe-matched value, so tuning until it vanishes over-smooths. On this scan the true fringe (0.21%) is also below the pixel noise, so there is no reliable visual endpoint. Start from the measured value instead: δ/β ≈ 580 at an assumed 30 keV.
  5. Unverified Carry the value over to NRecon's scale. The same filter needs δ/β × E / 30 keV in a program that assumes energy E (about 390 at 20 keV). If NRecon does not state the energy it assumes, the factor is unknown: bracket the value in 2× steps and compare each preview with the no-filter preview.
  6. Go above the matched value only for the noise level a segmentation needs (Test 3 table); beyond it the filter only smooths. On each preview, check the finest structures: if thin cuticle lines blur or edges smear into the air, lower δ/β.
  7. Inference Set NRecon's separate Smoothing option to 0 while phase retrieval is on. Both are low-pass filters, so using both smooths twice. We did not test how NRecon combines them.
  8. Reconstruct the full stack and report δ/β, and the assumed energy if NRecon shows one, in the methods section.
For this fly dataset Values on our 30 keV scale; multiply by E / 30 keV for a program that assumes energy E.
  • Segmenting soft tissue (flight muscle, gut, fat body): δ/β ≈ 1600. CNR rises from 30 to about 110.
  • Fine cuticle structure (setae, thin sclerites, wing veins): no phase retrieval, or a weak one (≈ 600) with a sharper reconstruction window.
  • Both: reconstruct twice, segment tissue on the phase-retrieved volume and measure cuticle on the standard one. Both come from the same projections, so they are aligned slice for slice.
For future scans where phase contrast matters
  • Inference Lower the Fresnel number: no camera binning when the field of view allows, and a lower tube voltage (about 40–60 kV), which also raises absorption contrast in insect tissue. Phase retrieval gains most on high-resolution scans of low-density samples.
  • Keep the full number of projections. Phase retrieval does not make up for streaks from too few angles (Test 6).
Part 6 · Reference

The math, in plain words

M = CS / OS
In the code: geometry()

Magnification. CS is the source-to-camera distance and OS the source-to-sample distance. If the camera is 5 times farther from the source than the sample is, the shadow of the sample is 5 times larger on the camera.

z_eff = OS × (CS − OS) / CS
In the code: geometry()

Effective propagation distance. (CS − OS) is the gap between sample and camera, where fringes grow. Because the beam is a cone, the image is also magnified on the way, so the gap is scaled down to what it would be at the sample's own size. A longer z_eff means stronger fringes.

I_camera = I_absorption + z_eff × (how sharply the sample's "phase thickness" curves)
In the code: the "camera" loop inside render(); physics name: transport-of-intensity equation

Where fringes come from. δ measures how much a material bends X-rays. Adding up δ along each ray gives a "phase thickness". Where that thickness changes direction sharply, at an edge, X-rays pile up on one side (bright line) and leave the other (dark line). The code measures this curvature by comparing each camera pixel with its two neighbours.

β = μ × λ / (4π)    so    δ/β = δ × 4π / (μ × λ)
In the code: TISSUE_DB constant

The δ/β ratio. β is the absorption of the material written in the same units as δ. μ is the usual attenuation coefficient and λ the X-ray wavelength (0.062 nm at the 20 keV used in the simulator). δ/β is "how much it bends" divided by "how much it absorbs". For soft tissue it is in the thousands.

filtered = original ÷ (1 + c × (2π / period)²),   c = z_eff × (δ/β) × λ / (4π)
In the code: the HP filter array in render(), applied with applyFilter(); flyFilter() in two dimensions

The Paganin filter (Paganin et al., 2002). Each projection is split into patterns of different sizes ("periods"). Large patterns are divided by almost exactly 1 and stay the same. Small patterns are divided by a large number and shrink. Edge fringes are the smallest patterns, so they are removed. Random pixel noise is also fine-scale, so it drops too.

L = √c
In the code: geometry(), shown as "Smoothing length"

Smoothing length (also called the Paganin length). The size, in µm, below which the filter starts removing detail. Compared with the pixel size, an L of several pixels means a strong filter. For the fly scan, the fringe-matched δ/β ≈ 580 gives L ≈ 9 µm, about one pixel.

N_F = p² / (λ × z_eff)
In the code: geometry(), shown as "Fresnel number"

Fresnel number. p is the pixel size. Values above about 1 mean the fringes are thin compared with a pixel, the regime the Paganin method and this simulation assume. Larger values mean weaker visible fringes: the fly scan has N_F ≈ 37.

projection = −ln(I)  →  sharpen (ramp filter)  →  smear back across the image at every angle
In the code: rampH, applyFilter(), backproject()

Reconstruction. Taking −ln turns transmitted intensity into total attenuation along the ray. Each projection is then sharpened and spread back over the image along its viewing angle. The simulator adds 180 angles; NRecon and our case-study reconstruction do the same with the cone-beam version of the algorithm (FDK).

Part 7 · Limits and sources

What is verified and what is not

From Bruker's distributor (Blue Scientific), via search results
  • NRecon 2.0 includes Paganin single-distance phase retrieval.
  • It works on existing scans if the projection images are still available.
  • Many parameters are read from the scan .log; the key user setting is δ/β.
  • Step-by-step guide: Bruker method note MN133. It is not in this repository's manuals folder.
Measured on the fly scan
  • Part 2 uses projection 531 with this page's own Paganin filter, not NRecon's.
  • Parts 3 and 4 use our own GPU reconstruction of all 1800 projections. It reproduces NRecon's geometry exactly and its noise level away from the pin, but has no beam-hardening correction, and its absolute grey values are on a different scale.
  • "Detail kept" is measured against the unfiltered reconstruction, not against the true fly. Four slices were measured in detail, with consistent results.
Not verified
  • NRecon's own phase-retrieval output: we did not run it, so the factor between its δ/β scale and ours is unknown, as are the exact menu names.
  • The .log field names on other systems. The parser looks for the names shown under each slider.
  • The simulator's default distances (55 mm and 275 mm) are example values, not 1272 specifications. Its material values are approximate textbook values at a single energy of 20 keV, and it is simplified: parallel beam instead of cone beam, one energy, one detector-blur value, 180 projections.
  • One scan of one sample. A sample or setup with stronger phase contrast could benefit far more.

Questions for discussion

1. Why does the standard deviation of (even − odd) / 2 estimate the noise of the full reconstruction?

Each half uses half the photons, so its noise variance is 2σ², where σ² is the variance of the full reconstruction. The halves are independent, so even − odd has variance 4σ², and dividing by 2 gives σ². The fly itself is identical in both halves and cancels.

2. Two linear filters with the same output noise are applied to the same data. Why must they keep similar detail?

Output noise is set by how much each filter passes at every frequency, weighted by the noise spectrum. Matching the noise therefore largely fixes how much the filter passes overall, and with it how much signal survives. Only the distribution across frequencies can differ, which here was a small effect. Physics enters only through what one filter removes that the other does not; here, the fringe.

3. The pixel size is halved to 4 µm, with everything else unchanged. What happens to the Fresnel number, the fringe and the matched δ/β?

NF scales with p², so it falls about fourfold to roughly 9 and the fringe becomes more visible. The matched δ/β is a property of the material and the spectrum, so it should stay near the same value. The smoothing length stays about 9 µm, which is now about two pixels rather than one, so the filter removes relatively more of what the finer pixels can resolve.

4. The fringe is cancelled at δ/β ≈ 580, but the dark halo in the slices disappears only near 3200. What does that suggest?

Most of the halo in the reconstructed slices is not phase contrast. Plausible contributors are the edge response of the ramp filter, partial-volume effects and beam hardening. Beyond δ/β ≈ 580 the filter reduces the halo by blurring it, as Gaussian smoothing also does.

5. The single head edge in Part 2 needed δ/β ≈ 2000, but the average edge needed 580. Which is right?

Paganin's method assumes one material, so one δ/β cannot match every edge. The sharp head edge has the strongest fringe in the scan; the average over many clean edges represents the sample as a whole. Tuning on the most striking edge over-smooths everything else, which is why we measure the fringe over many edges rather than judging it by eye on one.

6. Halving the number of projections raised noise 2.4× instead of 1.4×. Where does the extra come from?

Angular undersampling. With fewer views, structures produce streaks that do not average out. Our noise measure counts the streaks that differ between the even and odd halves, which inflates the number further. Phase retrieval lowers random noise but does not restore missing angles.
