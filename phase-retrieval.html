Your geometry

The SkyScan 1272 has a small X-ray spot and no gratings. X-rays bend slightly at boundaries inside the sample, and over the gap between the sample and the camera that bending turns into bright and dark lines along every edge. That gap is what the phase effect depends on.

What the camera records

One line of one projection image across the simulated sample. Tissue absorbs only a few percent at these energies, so the absorption signal is faint. The edge fringes are often larger than the absorption itself.

Why one number fixes it

Phase effects boost fine detail: the smaller the feature, the stronger the boost (amber). The Paganin filter reduces fine detail by the opposite amount (teal). When δ/β matches the material, the two cancel and the result is flat (black). Set it too low and halos survive; set it too high and real detail is smoothed away.

Doing it on your data

  1. Check that the scan folder still has the raw projection images and the matching .log. Phase retrieval works on projections, so reconstructed slices alone are not enough.
  2. Open the dataset in NRecon 2.0 or newer. Bruker's distributor describes phase retrieval as new in 2.0. Check the version on your reconstruction PC.
  3. Turn on phase retrieval and enter δ/β. The distances and pixel size come from the .log automatically. Unverified I could not confirm the menu name; Bruker method note MN133 has the walk-through.
  4. Preview one slice. If bright or dark halos remain at edges, raise δ/β. If fine structures blur, lower it. Changing it in steps of 2–3× is a practical way to find the range.
  5. Inference Phase retrieval already smooths, so reduce NRecon's separate Smoothing setting to avoid smoothing twice.
  6. Reconstruct the full stack and write the δ/β value in your methods section.
For future scans Inference On this scanner, a smaller pixel size (higher magnification) makes phase fringes stronger relative to pixel size, so phase retrieval gains the most on high-resolution scans of low-density samples.

The math, in plain words

M = CS / OS
In the code: geometry()

Magnification. CS is the source-to-camera distance and OS the source-to-sample distance. If the camera is 5 times farther from the source than the sample is, the shadow of the sample is 5 times larger on the camera.

z_eff = OS × (CS − OS) / CS
In the code: geometry()

Effective propagation distance. (CS − OS) is the gap between sample and camera, where fringes grow. Because the beam is a cone, the image is also magnified on the way, so the gap is scaled down to what it would be at the sample's own size. Longer z_eff means stronger fringes.

I_camera = I_absorption + z_eff × (how sharply the sample's "phase thickness" curves)
In the code: the "camera" loop inside render(); physics name: transport-of-intensity equation

Where fringes come from. δ measures how much a material bends X-rays. Adding up δ along each ray gives a "phase thickness". Where that thickness changes direction sharply (at an edge), X-rays pile up on one side (bright line) and leave the other (dark line). The code measures this curvature by comparing each camera pixel with its two neighbours.

β = μ × λ / (4π)     so    δ/β = δ × 4π / (μ × λ)
In the code: TISSUE_DB constant

The δ/β ratio. β is the absorption of the material written in the same units as δ. μ is the usual attenuation coefficient and λ is the X-ray wavelength (0.062 nm at the 20 keV used here). δ/β is "how much it bends" divided by "how much it absorbs". For soft tissue it is in the thousands, so tissue bends X-rays far more than it absorbs them.

filtered = original ÷ (1 + c × (2π / period)²),   c = z_eff × (δ/β) × λ / (4π)
In the code: the HP filter array in render(), applied with applyFilter()

The Paganin filter. Each projection is split into patterns of different sizes ("periods"). Large patterns are divided by almost exactly 1 and stay the same. Small patterns are divided by a large number and shrink. Edge fringes are the smallest patterns, so they are removed. Random pixel noise is also fine-scale, so it drops too.

L = √c
In the code: geometry(), shown as "Smoothing length"

Smoothing length. The size, in µm, below which the filter starts removing detail. Compare it with your pixel size: an L of several pixels means a strong filter.

N_F = p² / (λ × z_eff)
In the code: geometry(), shown as "Fresnel number"

Fresnel number. p is the pixel size. Values above about 1 mean the fringes are thin compared with a pixel. That is the regime the Paganin method and this simulation assume.

projection = −ln(I)  →  sharpen (ramp filter)  →  smear back across the image at every angle
In the code: rampH, applyFilter(), backproject()

Reconstruction. Taking −ln turns transmitted intensity into "total attenuation along the ray". Each projection is then sharpened and spread back over the image along its viewing angle. Adding all 180 angles rebuilds the slice. NRecon does the same in principle, with a cone-beam version of the algorithm.

What is verified and what is not

From Bruker's distributor (Blue Scientific), via search results
  • NRecon 2.0 includes Paganin single-distance phase retrieval.
  • It works on existing scans if the projection images are still available.
  • Many parameters are read from the scan .log; the key user setting is δ/β.
  • Step-by-step guide: Bruker method note MN133. It is not in this repository's manuals folder.
Not verified
  • The exact NRecon menu names, and whether NRecon's δ/β field equals the physical ratio or is a scaled version.
  • The .log field names on your system. The parser looks for the names shown under each slider.
  • The default distances (55 mm and 275 mm) are example values, not 1272 specifications.
  • Material values are approximate textbook values at a single energy of 20 keV. Your scanner produces a spread of energies, so the best δ/β on real data is found by eye.
  • The simulation is simplified: parallel beam instead of cone beam, one energy, one detector-blur value, 180 projections.
